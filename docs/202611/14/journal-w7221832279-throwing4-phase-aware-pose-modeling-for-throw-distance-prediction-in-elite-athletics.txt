Abstract 

Elite athletics throwing provides a structured biomechanical action sequence together with an objective continuous outcome, namely throw distance, making it a suitable setting for performance-oriented sports analytics. In this work, we investigate whether explicit phase alignment of pose dynamics improves throw-distance prediction under realistic broadcast conditions. To support this study, we curate Throwing4, a dataset of 500 elite throwing clips spanning javelin, dis-cus, hammer, and shot put, with corresponding two-dimensional pose sequences, frame-level phase annotations, and official distance labels. We formulate throw-distance prediction from broadcast-derived pose as a regression problem and compare full-sequence modeling against a phase-conditioned approach that par-titions each attempt into four technique stages and fuses phase-specific motion representations. Experiments across multiple graph-based pose encoders show a consistent reduction in prediction error when phase structure is incorporated, indicating that phase-aligned modeling provides a more stable representation than treating the entire motion as an unstructured sequence. These findings sug-gest that explicit biomechanical phase information is a useful inductive prior for pose-based performance modeling in elite athletics throwing. Beyond score

# 1 Introduction 

Computer vision and artificial intelligence (AI) are increasingly used in sports anal-ysis to convert raw video into measurable information about technique, timing, and performance. Modern pipelines can track athletes, estimate body keypoints, detect critical events, localize actions in time, and summarize movement patterns from realis-tic broadcast footage [1, 2]. This capability is important in sport because practitioners rarely need only a coarse action label; they also need to know when decisive moments occur and how the body moves through those moments. Large-scale datasets have been central to this progress. General-purpose video datasets such as UCF101 and Kinetics support broad representation learning [3, 4], while sports-oriented resources increasingly emphasize fine temporal structure, hierarchical action organization, and performance-sensitive supervision [5–7]. At the same time, sports video remains challenging because realistic broadcasts contain camera motion, zoom, changing view-points, motion blur, occlusion, clutter, and editing artifacts, all of which complicate temporally precise reasoning [1, 2]. This creates a persistent gap between the struc-tured movement descriptions that coaches and analysts care about and the supervision many existing datasets or models directly provide. A second challenge concerns how movement is measured. High-fidelity biome-chanics often relies on specialized instrumentation such as motion-capture systems, markers, force plates, or wearable sensors, which can provide accurate signals but are difficult to deploy at scale in real competitions. Markerless pose estimation from ordi-nary red–green–blue (RGB) video offers a practical alternative because it produces compact and interpretable joint trajectories without requiring controlled laboratory setups or athlete instrumentation [8, 9]. Once represented as skeletons, movement naturally forms a spatio-temporal graph in which body joints evolve over time, mak-ing graph-based models a natural choice for analysis [10–13]. This representation is well suited to sports in which technique is understood as an ordered sequence of phases rather than as a single indivisible motion. In such cases, explicitly incorpo-rating phase structure may improve interpretability and may reduce the influence of variable-duration content that is less informative for the final outcome [14, 15]. These issues are evident in athletic throwing. Throwing actions follow an ordered biomechanical progression, while performance is summarized by an objective con-tinuous outcome: the final throw distance. Elite competitions are a suitable setting for research because technique is more standardized, attempts are governed by clear rules, and outcome labels are officially recorded. However, despite recent progress in sports datasets and pose-based modeling, there is still no widely adopted resource 2that jointly aligns elite throwing broadcasts with identity-consistent pose tracks, frame-level phase boundaries, and official distance outcomes across multiple throwing disciplines. This motivates the present study. We formulate elite throwing score pre-diction from broadcast-derived pose as a phase-aware performance modeling problem, curate a multi-discipline dataset to support this study, and investigate whether phase-conditioned modeling provides a measurable advantage over full-sequence regression under realistic broadcast conditions. The main contributions of this work are as follows: 

• We formulate elite throwing score prediction from broadcast-derived pose as a phase-aware performance modeling problem and curate Throwing4 to support evaluation across four athletics disciplines. 

• We introduce a reproducible pose-based prediction protocol using identity-consistent two-dimensional (2D) pose trajectories in the 16-joint Max Planck Institute for Informatics layout (MPII-16), lightweight pose normalization, and evaluation in both normalized units and metres. 

• We study a phase-conditioned regression formulation that encodes each technique stage separately before fusion and compare it against full-sequence modeling across multiple graph-based pose encoders. 

• We show that phase-conditioned modeling consistently reduces prediction error under the present experimental protocol, suggesting that biomechanical phase struc-ture is a useful inductive prior for pose-based performance modeling in elite athletics throwing. 

# 2 Related Work 

2.1 Sports video datasets and benchmarks 

Progress in sports vision has been strongly shaped by datasets that combine real-istic viewing conditions with structured supervision. Generic action datasets such as UCF101 and Kinetics established strong foundations for video representation learning [3, 4], while sports-specific resources pushed the field toward domain-aware temporal reasoning. FineGym emphasizes hierarchical temporal structure [5], Soccer-Net supports large-scale event spotting in broadcast soccer [16, 17], and MultiSports addresses multi-person spatio-temporal action localization [18]. More recent action-quality datasets, such as FineDiving and LOGO, connect video understanding with procedure-aware or score-related assessment [6, 7]. These resources show a shift from coarse action classification toward temporally structured and performance-sensitive analysis. However, relatively few datasets jointly provide elite athletics throwing videos, pose trajectories, phase boundaries, and official continuous distance labels. 

2.2 Fine-grained temporal structure and phase supervision 

Many sports actions are better understood as ordered technique stages than as single holistic actions. The decomposable-motion perspective [14, 15] aligns with coaching practice, where diagnostically important cues occur in specific temporal segments. In athletics throwing, this staged structure is explicit: preparation, force generation, 3release, and recovery are not interchangeable parts of the motion. Prior work on javelin phase segmentation has shown that biomechanical phase boundaries can be detected from motion dynamics and can support temporally localized analysis [19]. Related unsupervised and weakly supervised segmentation methods also indicate that temporal embeddings, clustering, and transport-based alignment can recover structured action progression without dense labels [20–22]. These studies motivate the use of phase structure as both an interpretability mechanism and an inductive bias for performance modeling. 

2.3 Pose-based temporal modeling and biomechanical performance prediction 

Markerless pose provides a compact and interpretable representation for sports move-ment because it captures joint trajectories from ordinary video without requiring laboratory instrumentation. Pose-estimation toolkits such as MMPose make large-scale extraction feasible [9], and graph-based skeleton models naturally represent the body as a spatio-temporal joint graph [10–13]. For throwing, this representation is also biomechanically meaningful. Track-and-field throwing performance is known to depend on coordinated segmental motion, release velocity, release angle, release height, and technique consistency [23]. In javelin, recent kinematic studies relate release veloc-ity to run-up and body-segment contributions [24, 25]. In discus, release parameters are athlete-specific and strongly coupled to performance [26]. Hammer-throw studies emphasize the role of release velocity and rotational dynamics [27], while shot-put studies show that kinematic repeatability and technique-dependent variables influ-ence throwing distance [28]. These biomechanical findings support the central premise of this work: phase-aligned pose dynamics may provide a useful representation for predicting continuous throwing performance from broadcast-derived skeletons. 

# 3 Methods 

3.1 Dataset curation and annotation 

Throwing4 is designed as a resource for phase-aware performance modeling in elite athletics throwing under realistic broadcast conditions. It contains curated RGB clips of complete competition attempts, identity-consistent MPII-16 2D pose sequences extracted from each clip, frame-level phase boundaries, and the official throw distance associated with each attempt. The dataset spans four throwing disciplines—javelin, discus, hammer, and shot put—and is compiled from major international events, including the Tokyo 2020 and Paris 2024 Olympic Games, the World Athletics Cham-pionships (WAC) in Beijing 2015 and Oregon 2022, and the European Athletics Championships (EAC) 2024. We identify candidate attempts by scanning long-form broadcasts and then manu-ally review each candidate to ensure that it corresponds to a complete, valid, non-foul throw with sufficient visual observability for pose extraction and temporal annotation. 3.2 Pose extraction and identity-consistent thrower tracking 

To obtain scalable motion measurements from broadcast RGB, we extract a single identity-consistent skeleton sequence for the primary throwing athlete in each clip using a top-down pose estimation and tracking pipeline, illustrated in Fig. 2. Videos from all four disciplines are first processed with MMPose [9] for multi-person detec-tion and 2D keypoint estimation. For each frame It, the detector produces a set of candidate person bounding boxes Bt after confidence thresholding and non-maximum suppression. In realistic broadcast scenes, however, the frame often contains multiple people such as officials, camera operators, and nearby athletes, so naive frame-wise estimation over all detections can introduce identity switches and temporally inconsistent pose trajectories. To address this, we construct a single tracked bounding-box sequence bBt

for the thrower of interest using deterministic detection association. The track is initialized from the dominant athlete box, and subsequent detections are selected by maximizing spatial overlap with the previous target subject to a gating threshold that reduces drift under crowding, scale variation, and brief occlusion. Con-ditioned on bBt, MMPose produces MPII-format 2D skeletons with J = 16 joints per frame, yielding one temporally consistent MPII-16 pose sequence for each clip. This step converts a variable and cluttered multi-person broadcast scene into a compact athlete-centered motion representation suitable for temporal modeling and subsequent phase-aware regression.

# 4 Results 

Table 2 reports the normalized mean absolute error (MAE) for full-sequence and phase-conditioned inputs across the four throwing disciplines, while Fig. 4 presents the corresponding errors after conversion to metres using Eq. (11). Across all evaluated graph-based models and all four disciplines, the phase-conditioned setting produces lower prediction error than the corresponding full-sequence setting. For example, in javelin, the ASTGCN MAE decreases from 0 .2023 to 0 .1070, while in hammer it decreases from 0 .2645 to 0 .1364. The same overall pattern is observed in the metre-scale results shown in Fig. 4. The lowest phase-conditioned MAE differs across disciplines. AGCN obtains the lowest error for javelin (0 .0662), GCN for discus (0 .1160), ASTGCN for ham-mer (0 .1364), and GCN for shot put (0 .2055). Despite these differences in the best-performing backbone, the phase-conditioned input yields lower error than the cor-responding full-sequence input for every graph-based model evaluated in Table 2. Addi-tional repeated-seed and phase-boundary analyses are reported in Online Resource 1. The phase-boundary ablation compares the annotated four-phase partition (Manual-4) with four equal temporal windows (Uniform-4) under the same AGCN backbone and five random seeds. # 5 Discussion 

Phase-conditioned modeling lowered throw-distance error in every discipline and under every pose-graph backbone tested. The consistency matters more than the margin, because it indicates that the gain comes from the representation rather than from a 10 Fig. 4 MAE in meters for full-sequence and phase-conditioned score prediction across four throwing disciplines. Errors in meters are obtained by de-normalizing the normalized MAE using Eq. (11). 

particular architecture. A throw is a short sequence of functionally distinct actions, and a model that pools an entire clip must average across them. Decomposing the sequence first lets each segment contribute its own summary, so that information from the delivery is not diluted by a long and variable approach. The metre-scale reduction is largest for javelin, which fits this account, since the javelin run-up varies most across athletes and clips in duration, rhythm, and scene composition [24, 25]. The phase-boundary ablation separates two things that the main result combines: segmenting the sequence at all, and segmenting it at biomechanically aligned points. Both formulations use four pooled descriptors, so any difference between them is attributable to boundary placement alone. Little difference appears. For javelin, dis-cus, and hammer the gap between annotated and uniform boundaries falls within the across-seed variation, and the one event where the gap exceeds seed variability, shot put, favours the uniform windows. The reasonable conclusion is that temporal decom-position is the active ingredient and that boundary placement is second-order at this sequence length and label volume. For the rotational events this is expected, since dis-cus and hammer turns blend into one another and leave less for a boundary to align to [26, 27]. This has a practical reading for sports engineering. Competition broadcast footage is easier to obtain than laboratory motion capture and closer to the conditions under 11 which elite sport is actually observed [1, 2], and it carries enough information for performance-oriented modeling once the representation is structured. The phase anno-tations in Throwing4 earn their cost not by improving this particular regression but by making a class of questions askable at all: which phase carries the performance sig-nal, whether boundaries can be recovered automatically, and how staged movement relates to outcome across disciplines. Each of those requires labelled phases to study. Four limitations bound these claims. The pose is two-dimensional and esti-mated from broadcast video, so joint torques, implement dynamics, and true three-dimensional kinematics remain out of reach. The data covers valid elite non-foul attempts, which sharpens the labels but leaves generalization to novice athletes and training footage untested. Phase boundaries are hand-annotated, so the phase-conditioned results are an oracle-boundary benchmark rather than a deployable system. And because the clip-level partition described in Section 3.4 is not athlete-independent, these numbers do not speak to athlete-independent generalization. The metre-scale error should accordingly be read as coarse estimation, informative for attempt-level comparison and technique analysis but not a substitute for official measurement. Each limitation names its own next step: automatic phase detection to remove the oracle boundaries, athlete-independent protocols to test transfer, phase-wise and joint-level attribution to locate the signal within a throw, and multimodal or three-dimensional input to recover the biomechanics that two-dimensional pose cannot. 

# 6 Conclusion 

This paper introduced Throwing4 as a resource for studying throw-distance predic-tion from elite athletics throwing under realistic broadcast conditions. The dataset combines curated RGB clips, identity-consistent MPII-16 pose sequences, frame-level phase annotations, and official throw distances across javelin, discus, hammer, and shot put. Using this resource, we formulated throw-distance prediction from pose as a regression problem and compared full-sequence modeling with a phase-conditioned alternative that explicitly respects the staged structure of throwing technique. Across all evaluated disciplines and graph-based backbones, phase-conditioned modeling pro-duced lower prediction error than full-sequence regression under the present protocol. These results suggest that biomechanical phase structure is a useful inductive prior for pose-based performance modeling in elite athletics throwing and provide a basis for future work on phase-aware sports analytics from broadcast video.
