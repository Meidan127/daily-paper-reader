Abstract 

Tree ensembles are among the most accurate models for tabular data, yet the computation behind a single prediction is spread over hundreds of deci-sion rules. Feature attributions and counterfactuals explain such predictions through the influence of features or a change of the input, leaving open which of the thresholds applied by the trees a prediction responds to, while struc-tural explanations compress these rules and lose the trees behind them. To address this gap, we propose a local Decision Predicate Graph (local DPG), which gathers the rules executed for a sample into a verifiable record of the computation, with a class support that reproduces every explained predic-tion. From these rules we select the critical predicate, the executed predicate whose crossing is estimated to move the model most strongly toward a chosen class, which offers the user a concrete threshold and the trees that share it, without an external explainer. Across 16 datasets and four ensemble fam-ilies, it separates differently predicted neighbours better than random and nearest-threshold predicates. A single crossing recovers the true class in 46– 69% of wrong predictions, against 29–50% when the same rule is applied to the feature ranked first by TreeSHAP, a measure close to the selection criterion, and on planted faults it finds the faulty threshold in 46–71% of the resulting errors, against 6–21% at random. 1. Introduction 

Tree ensembles, such as random forests, bagging and gradient boosting, remain among the most accurate and widely deployed models for tabular data. Their predictions result from the aggregation of hundreds of decision trees, each of which routes the input through a sequence of threshold tests, so the computation behind a single prediction is distributed across the model. Local explanation methods aim to make this computation intelligible for one input at a time [1, 2, 3], and they serve different purposes, among them justifying a prediction, controlling what a model has learned, and improving it through an understanding of its behaviour [4]. Improvement is the use that practitioners report most often, since explanations are mainly employed to debug models [5, 6], and debugging begins with understanding why individual predictions fail [7, 8]. This paper addresses the first step of this purpose, the examination of individual predictions, and asks, for a trained ensemble and a given input, which of the predicates executed by the model, if crossed, would most change the prediction toward an alternative class. A prediction of a tree ensemble can be explained from three complemen-tary points of view. Feature attributions such as SHapley Additive exPlana-tions (SHAP) and Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) describe the influence of each input, quantifying how much it contributed to the output [9, 1, 10]. Counterfactual explanations move instead to the 

input space , where they describe a change of the input that would have pro-duced a different prediction [11, 12, 13, 14]. A third view looks at the logical structure of the model, that is, at the predicates and rules that the trees actually executed, and underlies the route-based and formal explanations of tree ensembles [15, 16, 17, 18]. These perspectives are not interchangeable, and a growing body of work shows how far they can diverge. A large attri-bution does not imply that a feature affects the prediction [19], the features ranked first by attribution methods are often neither necessary nor sufficient to change it [20, 21], and Shapley values may even rank irrelevant features 2above relevant ones [22, 23]. Counterfactuals, in turn, indicate what should change in the input, yet they do not single out a predicate of the model at which the prediction can be changed. This is the question that motivates the present work, and it is the structural view that can answer it. For a single decision tree, the logical structure of a prediction is easy to describe, since it coincides with the root-to-leaf route followed by the sample, a conjunction of predicates of the form xj ≤ θ or xj > θ that reads as a rule. A tree ensemble applies many such rules at once, one per tree, some of which support the predicted class while others support an alternative class. Existing structural explanations compress, select or aggregate these routes, and in doing so they lose three elements of this computation, namely the complete set of rules that were executed, the trees that share each predicate, and the rules that lead to competing classes. Preserving these elements makes it possible to ask where, among the executed predicates, a prediction can be changed. Our hypothesis is that, when the trees of an ensemble reuse the same split thresholds, crossing a single predicate shared by a group of trees can change the prediction in a substantial share of cases, even though the evidence for a competing class is spread over many predicates, and that this part of the model’s computation is made explicit neither by attributions nor by counterfactuals. We therefore propose a structural local explanation that follows the com-putation of the ensemble for one sample. A local Decision Predicate Graph 

(local DPG) gathers the executed rules into a single graph, built only from the paths of this sample rather than from a graph induced from the training data as in the original formulation of DPGs [18], and keeps on every node and edge the identity of the trees that executed it, while the class support is com-puted from the original contributions of the trees. From these rules we select the critical predicate , the executed predicate whose threshold, if crossed, is estimated to move the ensemble most strongly toward a chosen class, which addresses why the model predicted one class rather than another [24]. The contributions of this paper are organised in three levels, a structure that records the computation behind a prediction, an insight drawn from this structure, and its use on wrong predictions. 1. We propose the local DPG, which represents every predicate executed for a sample, records the trees that executed it and derives a class support that reproduced every explained prediction, at a cost of 87 to 461 ms per sample (Sections 3 and 5.4). The graph is too large to 3be read by a person, and we propose it as a verifiable record of the computation. 2. We define the critical predicate, the element offered to the user, which requires no external explainer. It exceeds random and nearest-threshold predicates on measures that do not involve its selection, and its cross-ing, which changes a single feature, alters the prediction more often than the same rule applied to the feature ranked first by TreeSHAP or LIME in most families (Section 5.1). 3. We show how the critical predicate helps to examine wrong predictions. A single crossing recovers the true class in 46–69% of cases, against 29–50% when the same rule is applied to the feature ranked first by contrastive TreeSHAP, and it locates faults planted in synthetic data (Section 5.2). The experiments cover 16 datasets and four ensemble families and are organised around four research questions, on how the critical predicate com-pares with other predicates (RQ1), wrong predictions (RQ2), training-free repair (RQ3), and cost and size (RQ4). They also mark the limits of this use, since training-free edits of the localised predicates do not reduce the error on unseen data (Section 5.3). Section 2 reviews related work through a taxonomy based on these three views. Section 3 formalises the executed traces of an ensemble and presents the local DPG, the critical predicate and its use. Section 4 describes the experimental design, and Section 5 reports the results for the four research questions. Section 6 discusses implications, scope and limitations, and Sec-tion 7 concludes the paper.

2.1. Structural explanations 

Because every root-to-leaf path of a tree is a conjunctive rule, the logical structure of an ensemble can be made explicit as a set of rules. These methods describe the knowledge that an ensemble ap-plies across the data, and none of them shows which predicates produced a particular prediction. Local structural methods start from the sample at hand. Collection of High Importance Random Path Snippets (CHIRPS) condenses the patterns that recur among the executed paths into a single rule [15], LionForests selects a reduced set of paths and summarises them as feature ranges [16], and Bel-latrex combines one representative rule per group of trees [34]. Selecting and compressing the routes discards their structure and the paths that support competing classes, which is precisely the information a contrastive analysis requires. Formal explanations bring logical guarantees instead. Abductive explanations identify a subset-minimal set of feature values that entails the prediction, and can be computed for random forests with Boolean satisfiabil-ity (SAT) solvers [17], majoritary reasons relax minimality in exchange for tractability [35], and sufficient, necessary and complete reasons have been formalised [37]. Their contrastive duals identify a minimal set of features whose change can alter the prediction [36, 17]. All of them take the form of sets of conditions rather than routes and do not single out an executed predicate. Bipolar argumentation frameworks, finally, represent the support and attack relations among the trees of a forest [38], with arguments de-rived from the forest rather than from the predicates it executed. The local DPG belongs to this group, building the graph for a single prediction and equipping it with a class support and the critical predicate. 

2.2. Influence and local surrogates 

Feature attributions assign to each feature a contribution to the output. TreeSHAP computes SHAP values exactly for tree models in polynomial time [9, 1], and feature contributions accumulate the change in class proba-bility along each executed path [39]. Both summarise this information per feature, recording neither the predicates that were executed nor the trees that supported a competing class. Several studies have questioned how far such scores explain a prediction. Large weights need not affect the decision a model makes [19], the importance of a feature differs from its influence rela-tive to a reference value [46], top-ranked features are often neither necessary nor sufficient for a change of outcome [20, 21], and Shapley values can rank 7provably irrelevant features above relevant ones [22, 23]. Our evaluation bor-rows this vocabulary only operationally, asking whether the counterfactual found by feature tweaking crosses a predicate and whether the single-feature crossing of the predicate changes the prediction. Local surrogates describe the model around the sample. LIME fits a sparse linear model [10], Anchors searches for a rule under which the pre-diction remains fixed with high precision [40], and the LOcal Rule-based Explanations (LORE) method learns a decision tree on a synthetic neigh-bourhood, from which it extracts factual and counterfactual rules [14, 41]. Being model-agnostic, they describe how the model behaves near the sam-ple, and their rules are not statements about the predicates the model itself executed. Rule sets produced by such model-agnostic extractors have been shown to differ considerably when compared through quantitative metrics [47]. 

2.3. Counterfactuals and robustness 

Feature tweaking considers every tree path that leads to the desired class, computes the smallest change that satisfies it and retains the cheapest change that alters the prediction of the ensemble [12]. Flexible Optimizable Coun-terfactUal explanations for tree enSembles (FOCUS) pursues the same goal through a differentiable approximation [42], and mixed-integer programming finds provably optimal counterfactuals [13]. The robustness of tree ensembles raises a closely related question, for which Kantchelian et al. [43] compute the nearest instance that receives a different prediction and Chen et al. [44] ver-ify the minimal adversarial perturbation or a guaranteed lower bound on it. These methods search the input space for a small change, which may involve several features, and some offer guarantees on its size. The critical predicate asks a narrower question, restricted to one feature and to the thresholds the trees executed for the sample, and it returns an executed threshold and the trees that share it, without a guarantee of minimality. 

3. Proposed approach 

The approach follows the computation of the ensemble for one sample in five steps, illustrated in Figure 2. First, each tree executes one root-to-leaf rule for the sample (panel a). Second, the support of each class is computed from the original contributions of the trees, so that it reproduces the output of the ensemble (panel b). Third, the rules are gathered into a local graph 8that keeps, on every node and edge, the identity of the trees that executed it (panel c). Fourth, among the executed predicates, we select the one whose threshold, if crossed, is estimated to move the ensemble most strongly toward a chosen class (panel d). Fifth, the feature of that predicate is moved just past its threshold and the whole ensemble is evaluated again, which shows how the prediction responds (panel e).

3.2. Local DPG 

Each trace πb(x) defines a decision rule whose antecedent is the conjunc-tion of its predicates and whose consequent is its outcome. The local DPG 

Gx represents the rules executed for x as a single directed acyclic graph, with a source node, predicate nodes and one sink node for each class. Each trace induces a path from the source through its predicates to the sink of its out-come, and every node and edge is labelled with the set of trees whose traces traverse it. Merging predicates on their labels alone could create source-to-sink paths that no tree executed, by joining the beginning of one trace to the end of another. A predicate node is therefore identified by its label together with the labels that immediately precede it on the trace, and the number of preceding labels is the smallest for which every source-to-sink path, or 

route , coincides with the label sequence of an executed trace. This condition is checked exactly on the traces of the sample, and keeping the whole prefix of every trace always satisfies it. Unlike the original DPG, which is induced from the training data, Gx is induced from the traces of x alone, so that its 11 routes enumerate the decision rules the model applied to x and its tree sets identify the trees that applied them (Figure 2c). 

3.3. Critical predicate 

Explanations are often sought in contrastive form, asking why one out-come occurred rather than another [24]. Intuitively, the critical predicate is the executed predicate whose threshold, if crossed, is estimated to move the ensemble most strongly toward a chosen target class c.To cross an executed predicate p = ( j, θ, ◦) is to move xj to the other side of θ. The crossing value ˜xj is the value closest to θ on that side, in the precision in which the trees compare features, and the slack of the predicate, the margin by which the sample satisfies its inequality, is |xj − θ|. A critical predicate toward the top competitor existed in 97.7% to 100.0% of the samples, depending on the family. Because the gain sums over the trees that execute the same predicate, the critical predicate tends to be shared by groups of trees, and it usually lies on routes that reach the predicted class, since only the trees that do not yet support the target can move toward it, 12 as in Figure 2, where x8 ≤ 0.2 redirects T3 and T5. 3.4. Using the critical predicate 

The critical predicate is used by intervening on the whole model. Given a target class c, the threshold crossing of p∗(c) changes a single feature of the sample, setting xj to the crossing value of the threshold nearest to the sample among the trees that share the label, and evaluates every tree again on the crossed sample x′. We record the change in the support of the target class, 

∆P (c) = Sx′ (c) − Sx(c), and whether the predicted class changes, which we call a flip . This whole-model intervention changes one feature, not one node of the ensemble, so any tree whose route tests xj between the old and the new value also changes its route, whether or not it executed the predicate. It plays no role in the selection of p∗(c), and throughout the paper crossing a predicate refers to this intervention. The target determines the question that the intervention answers. With the top competitor, c = c†, the critical predicate indicates where the predic-tion is estimated to be most contested. When the true class y of a wrongly predicted sample is known, the target c = y asks whether a single threshold crossing turns the prediction into the true class, and we say that the predi-cate recovers the true class when the model then predicts y. The two targets differ in multiclass problems whenever the true class is not the strongest competitor.

4.1. Datasets and models 

We use 16 public numeric classification datasets, obtained from OpenML [48] and scikit-learn and most of them originating in the UCI repository [49], with 4 to 617 features and 2 to 26 classes (Table S15 of 14 the supplementary material). Each dataset is divided once, by stratified sampling, into a held-out test set of 20% of the samples and a remainder, which is split in the same way into a training core and a validation set in the proportion 80:20. We study four ensemble families of 100 estimators, random forests (RF), bagging of decision trees (Bagging), AdaBoost with Stagewise Additive Modeling using a Multi-class Exponential loss, SAMME (AdaBoost) and gradient boosting (GBM). For each combination of dataset, family and repetition, a single configuration is chosen on validation accuracy alone, among maximum depths of 4, 8 and unlimited for RF and Bagging, 1, 2 and 3 for AdaBoost, and 2, 3 and 5 for GBM with a learning rate of 

0.1, and refitted on the training and validation data. This refitted model is the one explained by every method and control, no explanation metric enters the selection, the local DPG has no hyper-parameter to tune, and the 10 repetitions differ in the random initialisation of the models and of the random controls. The code, the fixed splits and the per-sample results are archived on Zenodo [50]. 

4.2. Explanation methods and controls 

The baselines are TreeSHAP [1] in its path-dependent form, LIME [10], Anchors [40], LORE [14, 41] and feature tweaking [12], which returns the cheapest change of the sample, in standardised Euclidean distance, that sat-isfies the path to a leaf of the target class and changes the prediction of the whole model. Because of their cost, Anchors and LORE explain the first 50 test samples in the first three repetitions, and Anchors is reported for the 14 datasets within the memory limit, whereas all other methods explain every test sample in all 10 repetitions (Section S1 of the supplementary material). Each control selects a single executed predicate of the sample, which is then crossed exactly as the critical predicate is (Figure 3). Three controls are structural, namely a random executed predicate, the predicate with the smallest slack ( nearest ) and the predicate executed by the largest number of trees ( frequent ). The other five are guided by an explainer. Explainers score features or propose input changes but do not return a predicate, so each control takes the feature that its explainer ranks first (Section S1 of the sup-plementary material) and selects an executed predicate on that feature. The predicate nearest to the sample gives the TreeSHAP-guided (LIME-guided, and so on) predicate, and the predicate with the largest estimated gain gives its gain-guided counterpart, which applies the rule of the critical predicate to the feature indicated by the explainer. 4.3. Metrics 

Agreement with the model output is a necessary but weak criterion, since an explanation may match a prediction while misrepresenting how it was reached [51, 52, 47, 2], and explainers frequently disagree for the same in-put [53]. Fidelity is therefore guaranteed by construction, and the critical predicate is assessed first on two measures that do not involve its selection. The separation contrast is, among the 10 nearest training points whose pre-diction differs from that of the sample and the 10 nearest whose prediction is the same, the proportion of the former minus the proportion of the latter that lie on the other side of the threshold. The agreement with the counterfactual records whether the counterfactual found by feature tweaking, the cheapest among the candidates it examines, crosses the threshold. These measures and the flip rate are operational counterparts of the necessity and sufficiency discussed in Section 2 [20, 21], not formal tests of them. For RQ1, with the top competitor as target, we then measure the change 

∆P (c†) and the flip rate under the whole-model intervention, which test whether the estimated gain carries over to the model, together with the distance moved by the feature in standard deviations of the training data, 16 the number of trees that share the predicate, and whether the crossed sample is atypical , that is, whether its distance to the nearest training point exceeds the 95th percentile of that distance among training points. As a reference, an oracle selects the executed predicate with the largest change in the whole model. For RQ2, the target of every wrong prediction is its true class y, and the TreeSHAP control uses the largest absolute entry of the contrastive vector 

ϕ(ˆ y) − ϕ(y) (for binary gradient boosting, the largest |ϕj |). We measure the 

true-class recovery , the proportion of wrong predictions whose true class is recovered, and the two measures above, with the separation contrast com-puted among the training points labelled with the true and with the predicted class. A fault planted in the data provides a ground truth that real datasets lack, so we also generate ten standard normal features whose first four deter-mine the class through a noisy linear function, and plant in the training and validation labels either a faulty rule, with labels flipped where x4 > 0.3 and 

x5 > 0.3, or a region of label noise, with labels flipped with probability 0.8 where x4 > 0.8. Test labels are clean, so wrong test predictions inside the region are largely caused by the fault. Over 10 generated datasets per fault and family, we record how often a control selects a feature of the fault and how often its threshold also lies within 0.25 of the threshold of the fault. For RQ3, the validation errors of a model fitted on the training core are treated as errors observed in use. Each error nominates one executed predicate, chosen by the critical predicate, by the votes of the trees that predicted the wrong class ( vote ), by contrastive TreeSHAP or at random. The most nominated predicates are edited without retraining, by neutralising the leaves, trees or rules that depend on them or by moving their threshold, and an edit is kept only if it reduces the errors on a held-out half of the validation set (Section S2 of the supplementary material). The measure is the relative change of the test error. For RQ4, we measure the time per explanation and its size in its own units, and a scaling study relates the local DPG to the number of trees (Section S3 of the supplementary material).

5.3. RQ3, repair 

The predicates identified on wrong predictions did not help training-free edits reduce the error on unseen data. Few edits passed the held-out test (0.1 to 0.8 per repetition for the critical-predicate localisation), their effect on the test error lies between -0.6% and +0.5%, and none of the 42 comparisons with the vote, TreeSHAP and random localisations is significant after Holm’s correction, whereas refitting the model on the training and validation data reduces the test error by 11% to 15% for RF, Bagging and GBM, although it increases it by 8.5% for AdaBoost (Section S2 of the supplementary material). A likely reason, which we did not test, is that a small number of validation errors provides too little evidence to revise a predicate shared by many trees without disturbing the samples those trees predict correctly. 24 5.4. RQ4, cost and size 

Building the local DPG with its contrastive view takes 87 to 461 ms per sample (Figure 5), of the same order as TreeSHAP and LIME for RF and Bagging and up to 11 times slower than TreeSHAP for GBM and AdaBoost, whereas Anchors and LORE are 35 to 504 times slower than the local DPG, and it grows roughly linearly with the number of trees (Table S4 of the supplementary material). The graph itself is large, with 173 to 885 nodes on average, and its compact contrastive view still needs 26 to 123 predicates to retain 80% of the positive estimated gain, which indicates that the evidence for a competing class is spread over many predicates of small gain. 6. Discussion 

The critical predicate exceeds the random and nearest controls on mea-sures that do not involve its selection, and its estimated gain carries over to the whole model (RQ1). A single crossing recovers the true class of a wrong prediction in 46% to 69% of the cases (RQ2). The predicates it identifies did not help training-free edits reduce the error on unseen data (RQ3), and the local DPG is built in 87 to 461 ms per sample but is not compact (RQ4). 25 6.1. What the results show 

Frameworks for the qualities of a good explanation include the twelve properties of Nauta et al. [51] and the notions reviewed by Vilone and Longo [2], within the agenda set out by Longo et al. [3]. Along them, the strengths of the local DPG follow from its construction, since every route is an executed decision rule and the class support is the model’s own output (Table 7).

Knowing the most influential feature does not reveal which of its thresholds matters, whereas the estimated gain accumulates over all the trees that executed a predicate, which is consistent with the observation that influence and decision relevance are different notions [19, 20]. Directed toward the true class, the critical predicate names the threshold and the trees at which a wrong prediction can be changed, in binary and multiclass problems alike, which is a natural input to explanatory debugging, in which users correct a model through its explanations [8, 56]. 6.2. What the results do not show 

The critical predicate is not a minimal counterfactual, its crossing is a sizeable change of one feature, and its effect on the prediction is the quantity that its selection estimates, a control guided by feature tweaking reaching a similar effect once a counterfactual has been computed. On real data, a recovery shows that one crossing turns a wrong prediction into the true class, not that the predicate caused the error. Nor did the identified predicates support a repair without retraining, since locating a problem tells an analyst where to look, for example which rule a group of trees learned around which 27 threshold, but turning it into a better model requires more evidence than the few errors that reveal it [4]. The local DPG is not compact, and its usefulness to people, including the context, coherence and continuity of its explanations, was not evaluated. We therefore regard the graph as a verifiable record of the computation, in which rules, trees and competing routes can be inspected, and the critical predicate, which is selected from the traces themselves, as the element offered to a person. 

6.3. Limitations 

The critical predicate relies on predicates shared by several trees, and sharing varies widely. The average predicate is executed by 1.05 to 28.7 trees depending on the dataset and family (Table S14 of the supplementary mate-rial), and in random forests by only 1.05 to 2.37 trees outside iris. The flip rate rises with sharing in some families and not in others (Spearman corre-lation over datasets from 0.05 to 0.47), so the reuse of thresholds explains the effect only in part, and the result is not driven by datasets with discrete values such as digits, since without it the critical predicate still flips more often than the TreeSHAP-guided predicate on all 15 remaining datasets in every family. Ensembles that draw their thresholds at random rarely share a predicate and would require grouping thresholds into intervals. More gen-erally, the method is defined for classification with numeric features, and regression, categorical splits and other gradient-boosting variants would re-quire extensions. The repair experiment covers four operators and a single acceptance rule, runtimes were measured on a shared machine and indicate orders of magnitude only, and Anchors and LORE were evaluated on a subset of samples and repetitions. Finally, the properties that depend on users call for a human-centred evaluation, such as those that compare rule-based and example-based explanations [57, 58]. 

7. Conclusion 

This paper has addressed the logical structure behind a prediction of a tree ensemble. The local DPG represents the predicates the trees executed for a sample, records which trees executed each of them, and derives a class support that reproduces the prediction, and the critical predicate selects the executed predicate whose crossing is estimated to move the prediction most strongly toward a chosen class. 28 Across the four ensemble families studied, and without an external ex-plainer, the critical predicate exceeds random and nearest-threshold predi-cates on measures that do not involve its selection, and its estimated gain carries over to the whole model. A single crossing recovers the true class of a wrong prediction in 46–69% of cases, against 29–50% when the same rule is applied to the feature ranked first by contrastive TreeSHAP, and the crossed samples are about as atypical as counterfactuals. On planted faults it finds the threshold of the fault in 46–71% of the wrong predictions they cause. It therefore complements attributions and counterfactuals by naming a threshold and the trees at which a prediction responds to a single-feature change. Training-free edits of the predicates it localises did not reduce the error on unseen data. The graph is not compact and serves as a verifiable record of the computation, while the critical predicate is the element offered to the user. Several directions remain open, among them extensions to regression, cat-egorical splits and histogram-based gradient boosting, repairs that combine the localisation with additional data collected around the identified thresh-olds, the measurement of the continuity of explanations across similar inputs, and a user study addressing context, coherence and controllability.
